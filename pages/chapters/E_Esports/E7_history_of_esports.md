    <p>The <a class="popup-comment" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title='Examples of "eSports"' data-bs-content='For more thorough discussions of this history of esports, see:<br><br>Partin III, William Clyde. 2025. <a target="_blank" href="https://doi.org/10.17615/6dyz-ga73">“The E in Sports: The Platformization of Professional Gaming.”</a> PhD Dissertation, The University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill.<br><br>Taylor, T. L. 2012. <a target="_blank" href="https://direct.mit.edu/books/book/4420/Raising-the-StakesE-Sports-and-the"><i>Raising the Stakes: E-Sports and the Professionalization of Computer Gaming</i></a>. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press.'>typical history of esports</a> goes something like this: </p>
    <p>Esports <a class="popup-citation" onmouseover="myFunction(event)" onmouseout="myFunction(event)" href="https://direct.mit.edu/books/book/4420/Raising-the-StakesE-Sports-and-the" target="_blank">“can be traced back to even the earliest days of computer gaming.”<span class="popuptext">Taylor, T. L. 2012. <i>Raising the Stakes: E-Sports and the Professionalization of Computer Gaming</i>. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press: 3.</span></a> Records of competitive video gaming date back at least to 1972 with the so-called “<i>Spacewar</i> Olympics,” and the incorporation of multi-user high-score tracking to digital arcade machines in the mid-to-late 1970s baked competition into the DNA of even ostensibly “single player” games. Contributing to this was the fact that early video games were played in arcades, social spaces where it was common for people to gather around a single machine to watch someone demonstrate their prowess at a game. With the rise of home personal computers through the 1980s, video games migrated from the arcade into the home. Then, in the 1990s, networked multiplayer video games ushered in a new era of competitive gaming. Early multiplayer PC games such as <i>Doom</i> and <i>Quake</i> shifted the locus of play from many people gathered around a single arcade cabinet to many people sitting at their individual computers and competing in a fully online space. Conventions began springing up around these influential early titles, and dedicated gamers would gather to compete in LAN (local area network) parties - and at these conventions, prizes began to be awarded to the winning competitors. From the late 1990s into the early 2000s, these conventions gave rise to dedicated esports organizations with similar LAN party-styled structures: for example, the Cyberathlete Amateur League (CAL), the World Cyber Games (WCG), and <a class="popup-comment" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title='Major League Gaming' data-bs-content='For discussions of MLG, see:<br><br>Taylor, Nicholas Thiel. 2016. <a target="_blank" href="https://doi.org/10.1080/15295036.2016.1215481">“Now You’re Playing with Audience Power: The Work of Watching Games.”</a> <i>Critical Studies in Media Communication</i> 33(4):293–307.<br><br>Taylor, T. L. 2012. <a target="_blank" href="https://direct.mit.edu/books/book/4420/Raising-the-StakesE-Sports-and-the"><i>Raising the Stakes: E-Sports and the Professionalization of Computer Gaming</i></a>. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press.'>Major League Gaming (MLG)</a>. It is around this time that esports began to solidify as a distinct phenomenon; Marcella Szablewicz, for instance, locates the origin of <a class="popup-citation" onmouseover="myFunction(event)" onmouseout="myFunction(event)" href="https://doi.org/10.1177/1555412015595298" target="_blank">“the concept of e-sports as a professional sport”<span class="popuptext">Szablewicz, Marcella. 2015. “A Realm of Mere Representation? ‘Live’ e-Sports Spectacles and the Crafting of China’s Digital Gaming Image.” <i>Games and Culture</i> 11 (3): 259.</span></a> at the inaugural WCG in 2001.</p>
    <p>In 2002, Xbox Live launched. While at-home game consoles, distinct from home computers, had existed since the 1970s, Xbox Live enabled online play between consoles in a similar way to online play between PCs. With this, esports’ material interfaces began to diversify: competitors would play titles like <i>Starcraft</i> on PC and titles like <i>Halo</i> and <a class="popup-hyperlink" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title="Explore Further" data-bs-content="<a href='/Sonic-Unreal/call-of-duty'><i>Call of Duty</i></a>"><i>Call of Duty</i></a> on Xbox and Playstation consoles. The 2010s would see the rise of franchised esports leagues, similar to those of traditional sports, and a wholesale shift  towards PCs as the primary hardware. Where esports had previously been a vibrant but largely insular community, international <a class="popup-citation" onmouseover="myFunction(event)" onmouseout="myFunction(event)" href="https://doi.org/10.4324/9781315752174" target="_blank">“mega-events”<span class="popuptext">Borowy, Michael, and Dal Yong Jin. 2016. “Mega-Events of the Future: The Experience Economy, the Korean Connection, and the Growth of eSport.” In <i>Mega-Events and Globalization: Capital and Spectacle in a Changing World Order</i>, edited by Richard Gruneau and John Horne, 206–19. New York: Routledge.</span></a> (or even <a class="popup-citation" onmouseover="myFunction(event)" onmouseout="myFunction(event)" href="https://doi.org/10.1080/23750472.2025.2507225" target="_blank">“giga” events<span class="popuptext">Newham, Thomas, Nicolas Scelles, and Maurizio Valenti. 2025. “The Classification of Esports Events: Definitions, Sizes and Composite Index Development.” <i>Managing Sport and Leisure</i></span></a>) like <a class="popup-hyperlink" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title="Explore Further" data-bs-content="<a href='/Sonic-Unreal/league-of-legends'><i>League of Legends</i></a>"><i>League of Legends</i></a>’ Worlds and Dota 2’s The International began attracting over ten thousand in-person spectators and tens of millions of viewers worldwide. Facilitated through both the explosion of <a class="popup-hyperlink" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title="Explore Further" data-bs-content="<a href='/Sonic-Unreal/livestreaming'>Livestreaming</a>">livestreaming infrastructures (such as Twitch)</a> and an influx of venture capital (VC), esports ballooned as an international phenomenon throughout the 2010s. This was buoyed even further by the onset of the Covid-19 pandemic, with a popular assumption that, due to their ability to be played remotely in the midst of pandemic lockdowns, esports would reach an even wider viewership in the absence of traditional professional sports broadcasts and events. Indeed, in the US, mainstream networks such as NBC and ESPN broadcast esports  during the height of the pandemic, and traditional sports leagues like the MLB, MLS, and NHL put on esports events.</p>
    <p>But then the bubble popped, and in 2021-22 the <a class="popup-comment" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title='Esports Winter' data-bs-content='For more on the esports winter, see:<br><br>D’Anastasio, Cecilia. 2022. <a target="_blank" href="https://www.latimes.com/business/story/2022-12-08/esports-hype-fading-investors-sponsors-dry-up">“The Hype around Esports Is Fading as Investors and Sponsors Dry Up.”</a> <i>Los Angeles Times</i>, December 8.<br><br>Maas, Lea. 2024. <a target="_blank" href="https://esportsinsider.com/2023/11/what-is-esports-winter">“What Is the Esports Winter — and How to Navigate the Cold?”</a> <i>Esports Insider</i>, February 5.<br><br>Partin III, William Clyde. 2025. “Chapter 8. Epilogue: ‘Esports Keeps the Lights on’” in <a target="_blank" href="https://doi.org/10.17615/6dyz-ga73"><i>The E in Sports: The Platformization of Professional Gaming</i></a>. PhD Dissertation, The University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill.'>“esports winter”</a> began. While esports ecosystems had been robust and widespread for decades at this point, parts these infrastructures were unable to survive the glut of venture capital and pandemic-inspired public attention and their (perhaps inevitable) retraction a few years later. We are now in a moment where portions of the esports industry are severely consolidating, with esports organizations shuttering, prize pools shrinking, news outlets closing their esports departments, and jobs from players to coaches to support staff being lost. While the industry has by many metrics shrunk since the heyday of the late 2010s, it is still in some ways in a more robust position than before the influx of VC money. The esports winter has inspired a reconsideration of what esports can and should be: some corners of the industry are returning to older MLG-style tournament formats from the 2000s; video game streamers are beginning to fund teams and players in the absence of traditional sponsors; professional esports players are beginning to pseudo-unionize and stage walkouts for better working conditions.</p>
    <p>Esports is changing, but it is not going away. Poll industry commentators on where esports is headed, and the most common thing you will likely hear is that we are undergoing another shift in hardware norms along with an attendant shift in esports’ geographical center. PC-based esports which have long sat at the top of viewership charts, such as <a class="popup-hyperlink" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title="Explore Further" data-bs-content="<a href='/Sonic-Unreal/league-of-legends'><i>League of Legends</i></a>"><i>League of Legends</i></a> and <i>Counter-Strike</i>, are beginning to lose ground to mobile games such as <i>PUBG Mobile</i> and <i>Mobile Legends Bang Bang</i>. One mobile game, <i>Honor of Kings</i>, recently set the record for in-person attendance at an esports match with <a class="popup-citation" onmouseover="myFunction(event)" onmouseout="myFunction(event)" href="https://esportsinsider.com/2025/11/honor-of-kings-breaks-esports-attendance-guinness-world-record" target="_blank">over 62,000 fans<span class="popuptext">Daniels, Tim. 2025. “Honor of Kings Breaks Esports Attendance Guinness World Record.” <i>Esports Insider</i>, November 9.</span></a> gathering at the Bird’s Nest stadium in Beijing in November 2025. These games are particularly popular in East and Southeast Asia and, with their rising status, <a class="popup-citation" onmouseover="myFunction(event)" onmouseout="myFunction(event)" href="https://doi.org/10.1177/1555412015595298" target="_blank">countries such as China, Indonesia, and the Philippines are beginning to pull some economic and cultural influence away from traditional esports centers such as South Korea and the United States<span class="popuptext">Szablewicz, Marcella. 2015. “A Realm of Mere Representation? ‘Live’ e-Sports Spectacles and the Crafting of China’s Digital Gaming Image.” <i>Games and Culture</i> 11 (3): 263.</span></a>.</p>
    <p>This teleological narrative of esports’ evolution - from arcades to consoles to PCs to mobile - is only one possible story, however. Tara Fickle, for example, <a class="popup-citation" onmouseover="myFunction(event)" onmouseout="myFunction(event)" href="https://www.romchip.org/index.php/romchip-journal/article/view/132" target="_blank">has argued<span class="popuptext">Fickle, Tara. 2021. “Made in China: Gold Farming as Alternative History of Esports.” <i>ROMchip: A Journal of Game Histories</i> 3(1).</span></a> that esport <a class="popup-hyperlink" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title="Explore Further" data-bs-content="<a href='/Sonic-Unreal/playbor'>Playbor</a>">playbor</a> has its origins as much in <a class="popup-comment" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title='Gold Farming' data-bs-content='“Gold farming” refers to the practice of playing a game to earn in-game currency (“gold”) and then reselling it to other players for out-of-game currency. The practice, while common, is often seen as corrupting the play experience for “real” players and is racialized through the association of gold farming with countries in Central America and East/Southeast Asia.<br><br>For more on gold farming and examples of the discourses surrounding it, see:<br><br><i>The Economist</i>. 2019. <a target="_blank" href="https://www.economist.com/the-americas/2019/11/21/venezuelas-paper-currency-is-worthless-so-its-people-seek-virtual-gold">“Venezuela’s Paper Currency Is Worthless, so Its People Seek Virtual Gold,”</a> November 21.<br><br>Dibbell, Julian. 2006. <a target="_blank" href="https://www.hachettebookgroup.com/titles/julian-dibbell/play-money/9780465015368/?lens=basic-books"><i>Play Money: Or, How I Quit My Day Job and Made Millions Trading Virtual Loot</i></a>. New York: Basic Books.<br><br>Fickle, Tara. 2021. <a target="_blank" href="https://www.romchip.org/index.php/romchip-journal/article/view/132">“Made in China: Gold Farming as Alternative History of Esports.”</a> <i>ROMchip: A Journal of Game Histories</i> 3(1).<br><br>Nardi, Bonnie, and Yong Ming Kow. 2010. <a target="_blank" href="https://firstmonday.org/ojs/index.php/fm/article/download/3035/2566">“Digital Imaginaries: How We Know What We (Think We) Know about Chinese Gold Farming.”</a> <i>First Monday</i> 15(6).<br><br>Tai, Zixue, and Fengbin Hu. 2018. <a target="_blank" href="https://doi.org/10.1177/1461444817717326">“Play between Love and Labor: The Practice of Gold Farming in China.”</a> <i>New Media & Society</i> 20(7):2370–90.'>gold farming and the racialized discourses surrounding it</a> as in arcades. Alongside stories of technological progression and capital investment, esports’ history must be understood as influenced by <a class="popup-comment" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title='Gamergate' data-bs-content='St. James, Emily. 2014. <a target="_blank" href="https://www.vox.com/2014/9/6/6111065/gamergate-explained-everybody-fighting">“#Gamergate: Here’s Why Everybody in the Video Game World Is Fighting.”</a> <i>Vox</i>, October 13.<br><br>Hewa, Nelanthi, and Christine H. Tran. 2023. <a target="_blank" href="https://doi.org/10.1177/14614448231158387">“Verified Play, Precarious Work: GamerGate and Platformed Authenticity in the Cultural Industries.”</a> <i>New Media & Society</i> 26 (11): 6695–714.<br><br>Phillips, Amanda. 2020. <a target="_blank" href="https://nyupress.org/9781479834921/gamer-trouble/"><i>Gamer Trouble: Feminist Confrontations in Digital Culture</i></a>. New York: New York University Press.'>Gamergate and other cultural flashpoints in the history of video games more broadly</a>; <a class="popup-comment" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title='Military Ties to Esports' data-bs-content='During my fieldwork, for example, the U.S. National Guard sponsored the major esports organization NRG and the U.S. Navy sponsored the <i>Call of Duty</i> League.<br><br>For more on these relationships, see:<br><br>Michel, Pablo Nolla. 2023. <a target="_blank" href="https://doi.org/10.26503/dl.v2023i1.1918">“Gamers at War: The Relationship Between Military and Esports.”</a> In <i>Conference Proceedings of DiGRA 2023: Limits and Margins of Game Settings</i>.'>the ongoing history of military investments in and cultural ties with esports</a>; <a class="popup-hyperlink" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title="Explore Further" data-bs-content="<a href='/Sonic-Unreal/esports-and-geopolitics'>Esports & Geopolitics</a>">esports as a geopolitical tool of “sportswashing”</a>… and the list goes on.</p>
