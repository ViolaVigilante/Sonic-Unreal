    <div class="epigraph">
        <blockquote style="margin: 0;">
            <p>Reality ultimately is this virtual realm; a roiling ocean of pure processes that constantly mingle and clash. Our experiences of discrete things in space and time are nothing but distorted and inefficacious expressions of that virtual dimension.</p>
            <footer style="padding-bottom: 1.5%;"><a class="epigraph_source" target="_blank" href="https://euppublishingblog.com/2019/02/12/%EF%BB%BFgilles-deleuze-versus-process-philosophy/">Arjen Kleinherenbrink, “Gilles Deleuze versus Process Philosophy”</a></footer>
        </blockquote>
    </div>
    <p>	For philosophers working in <a class="popup-hyperlink" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title="Explore Further" data-bs-content="<a href='/Sonic-Unreal/affect-theory'>Affect Theory</a>" style="text-decoration: underline; font-weight: bold;">affect theory</a> and the Deleuzian metaphysical tradition, virtuality and reality are not opposed - <a class="popup-hyperlink" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title="Explore Further" data-bs-content="<a href='/Sonic-Unreal/virtuality-of-affect'>The Virtuality of Affect</a>" style="text-decoration: underline; font-weight: bold;">virtuality is, instead, <em>constitutive</em> of reality</a>. Breaking from <a class="popup-hyperlink" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title="Explore Further" data-bs-content="<a href='/Sonic-Unreal/analog-reality'>Analog Reality</a>" style="text-decoration: underline; font-weight: bold;">the commonplace notion that the virtual (read: digitally mediated) is outside of and opposed to the real (read: physical and material)</a>, scholars such as <a class="popup-citation" onmouseover="myFunction(event)" onmouseout="myFunction(event)" href="https://doi.org/10.2307/1354446" target="_blank">Brian Massumi&#9873;<span class="popuptext">Massumi, Brian. 1995. “The Autonomy of Affect.” <em>Cultural Critique</em> 31:83–109.</span></a> and <a class="popup-citation" onmouseover="myFunction(event)" onmouseout="myFunction(event)" href="https://www.versobooks.com/products/2339-the-antinomies-of-realism" target="_blank">Fredric Jameson&#9873;<span class="popuptext">Jameson, Fredric. 2013. <em>The Antinomies of Realism</em>. New York: Verso.</span></a> argue that the virtual undergirds the real.</p>
    <p>	It is unnecessary (I hope) to argue as I am writing this in 2026 that <a class="popup-hyperlink" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title="Explore Further" data-bs-content="<a href='/Sonic-Unreal/virtual-worlds-reality'>Virtual Worlds & Reality</a>" style="text-decoration: underline; font-weight: bold;">virtual worlds are themselves fundamentally real phenomena</a>. Such an argument is in any case fundamentally limiting, as it continues to imbue a hazy, ill-defined notion of “reality” with an ethical and ontological weight that belies any consensus on what that “reality” actually is. Instead, “The Sonic Unreal” explores virtuality as an aesthetic, discursive, and performative quality that is causally independent from reality. Neither does virtuality lead to reality in the Deleuzian sense nor does reality precede virtuality in <a class="popup-comment" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title='Edward Castronova' data-bs-content='Economist-turned-media-critic Edward Castronova, in his widely read work on virtual worlds and online video games, is one of the most public proponents of the notion that the “real world” - embodied, material, offline - is ontologically prior and, to a degree, ethically superior to “virtual worlds” - in video games and on the internet.<br><br><a target="_blank" href="https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/S/bo3620704.html">Castronova, Edward. 2005. <em>Synthetic Worlds: The Business and Culture of Online Games</em>. Chicago: University of Chicago Press.</a><br><br><a target="_blank" href="https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.5555/1522461">Castronova, Edward. 2007. <em>Exodus to the Virtual World: How Online Fun Is Changing Reality</em>. New York: Palgrave Macmillan.</a>'>the Castronovian sense<sup><b>&#8224;</b></sup></a>; for our purposes, links between virtuality and reality are omnipresent but heterogeneous, constructed for specific sociopolitical purposes rather than reflecting an inherent relationship. Virtuality sometimes indicates the presence of reality, and at other times it indicates the presence of unreality - but at all points it is indicative of some sort of relation with the concept of reality.</p>
