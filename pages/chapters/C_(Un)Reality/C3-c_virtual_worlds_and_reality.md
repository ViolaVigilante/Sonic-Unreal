            <p><a class="popup-hyperlink" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title="Explore Further" data-bs-content="<a href='/Sonic-Unreal/second-life'>Second Life</a>" style="text-decoration: underline; font-weight: bold;">Second Life</a> culture is profoundly human. It is not only that virtual worlds borrow assumptions from real life; virtual worlds show us how, under our very noses, our ‘real’ lives have been ‘virtual’ all along. It is being virtual that we are human: since it is human ‘nature’ to experience life through the prism of culture, human being has always been virtual being. Culture is our ‘killer app’: we are virtually human.</p>
            <footer style="padding-bottom: 1.5%;"><a class="epigraph_source" target="_blank" href="https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691168340/coming-of-age-in-second-life">Tom Boellstorff, <em>Coming of Age in Second Life</em></a></footer>

    <p><a class="popup-hyperlink" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title="Explore Further" data-bs-content="<a href='/Sonic-Unreal/virtual-worlds'>Virtual Worlds</a>" style="text-decoration: underline; font-weight: bold;">Virtual worlds</a>, by the very nature of their <a class="popup-hyperlink" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title="Explore Further" data-bs-content="<a href='/Sonic-Unreal/virtuality'>Virtuality</a>" style="text-decoration: underline; font-weight: bold;">virtuality</a>, may initially appear to be exterior or opposed to the real. This assumption - that <a class="popup-hyperlink" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title="Explore Further" data-bs-content="<a href='/Sonic-Unreal/virtuality-reality'>Virtuality & Reality</a>" style="text-decoration: underline; font-weight: bold;">virtuality is opposed to reality</a>, that <a class="popup-hyperlink" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title="Explore Further" data-bs-content="<a href='/Sonic-Unreal/analog-reality'>Analog Reality</a>" style="text-decoration: underline; font-weight: bold;">the analog is inherently more real than the digital</a> - obscures both that virtual worlds are fundamentally real in myriad ways and that the “real world” is itself composed of innumerable virtualities.</p>
    <div class="container">
        <div class="row">
            <div class="col-9">Early moral panics over virtual worlds (not only 3D-modeled online social spaces, but also <a class="popup-hyperlink" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title="Explore Further" data-bs-content="<a href='/Sonic-Unreal/history-of-virtual-worlds'>History of Virtual Worlds</a>" style="text-decoration: underline; font-weight: bold;">chatrooms and MUDs</a>) were caused not only by a sense that virtual worlds were themselves exterior to the real world, but also by fears that truth would cease to have meaning - or, at least, cease to be quantifiable - in these spaces. The famous 1993 <em>New Yorker</em> cartoon proclaiming that “On the Internet, nobody knows you’re a dog” fed on <a class="popup-hyperlink" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title="Explore Further" data-bs-content="<a href='/Sonic-Unreal/virtual-sex-panics'>Virtual Sex Panics</a>" style="text-decoration: underline; font-weight: bold;">deeper anxieties over identity and sex online</a>. With <a class="popup-hyperlink" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title="Explore Further" data-bs-content="<a href='/Sonic-Unreal/reality-and-truth'>Reality & Truth</a>" style="text-decoration: underline; font-weight: bold;">truth as itself a common measure of reality</a>, this doubled sense of unreality fed fears of a <em>Matrix</em>-style takeover of <a class="popup-hyperlink" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title="Explore Further" data-bs-content="<a href='/Sonic-Unreal/material-reality'>Material Reality</a>" style="text-decoration: underline; font-weight: bold;">physical reality</a> by <a class="popup-hyperlink" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title="Explore Further" data-bs-content="<a href='/Sonic-Unreal/virtuality-and-vr'>Virtuality & VR</a>" style="text-decoration: underline; font-weight: bold;">virtual reality</a>, or <a class="popup-citation" onmouseover="myFunction(event)" onmouseout="myFunction(event)" href="https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.5555/1522461" target="_blank">what Edward Castronova called an “exodus to the virtual world.”&#9873;<span class="popuptext">Castronova, Edward. 2007. <em>Exodus to the Virtual World: How Online Fun Is Changing Reality</em>.     <p>And yet, as has been well-established by <a class="popup-comment" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title='Scholarship on Virtual Worlds' data-bs-content='See, for example:<br><br><a target="_blank" href="https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691168340/coming-of-age-in-second-life?srsltid=AU7gw4XTax0ewgLQk4ExVU3MzBcMupvs5Eq021WO5BWRO06S4lcIwTCP">Boellstorff, Tom. 2008. <em>Coming of Age in Second Life: An Anthropologist Explores the Virtually Human</em>. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press.</a><br><br><a target="_blank" href="https://press.umich.edu/Books/M/My-Life-as-a-Night-Elf-Priest2">Nardi, Bonnie. 2010. <em>My Life as a Night Elf Priest: An Anthropological Account of World of Warcraft</em>. Ann Arbor, MI: University of Michigan Press.</a><br><br><a target="_blank" href="https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262516730/communities-of-play/">Pearce, Celia and Artemesia. 2009. <em>Communities of Play: Emergent Cultures in Online Games and Virtual Worlds</em>. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press.</a><br><br><a target="_blank" href="https://direct.mit.edu/books/oa-monograph/2800/Play-Between-WorldsExploring-Online-Game-Culture">Taylor, T. L. 2006. <em>Play Between Worlds: Exploring Online Game Culture</em>. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press.</a>'>decades of scholarship on virtual worlds<sup><b>&#8224;</b></sup></a>, virtual worlds are, in fact, fundamentally real: from the cultural reality of the social life within them, to the embodied reality of contact with the technological interfaces used to access them, to the economic reality of livelihoods made within them and corporate profits made from them, to the material reality of the oil burned and rare earth metals mined to make them function. The reality of virtual worlds is inarguable - but, crucially, this “reality” is fluid, heterogeneous, and oftentimes distinct from offline realities.</p>
