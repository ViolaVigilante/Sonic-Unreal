    <p>Queer Asianness within US empire, particularly queer Asian masculinity, has long been deeply and metonymically tied to food. The most striking example is the idea of the rice queen – <a class="popup-citation" onmouseover="myFunction(event)" onmouseout="myFunction(event)" href="https://nyupress.org/9780814759400/brown-boys-and-rice-queens/" target="_blank">“a gay Asiaphile from Euro-America whose primary attraction is the nubile, innocent brown boy.”&#9873;<span class="popuptext">Lim, Eng-Beng. 2013. <em>Brown Boys and Rice Queens: Spellbinding Performance in the Asias</em>. New York: New York University Press. ix.</span></a> The figure of the rice queen localizes the parasitic paternalism of US empire onto the Asiatic body, always already perceived as passive, submissive, and subservient. As Jodi Kim has shown, <a class="popup-citation" onmouseover="myFunction(event)" onmouseout="myFunction(event)" href="https://www.dukeupress.edu/settler-garrison" target="_blank">“the reigning trope figuring… long-standing US imperial interests and desires in Asia and the Pacific has been the gendered metaphor of sexual conquest or consummation.”&#9873;<span class="popuptext">Kim, Jodi. 2022. <em>Settler Garrison: Debt Imperialism, Militarism, and Transpacific Imaginaries</em>. Durham, NC: Duke University Press. 72.</span></a> The rice queen narrative takes shape as a direct result of American imperial projects across the Pacific, a narrative that <a class="popup-citation" onmouseover="myFunction(event)" onmouseout="myFunction(event)" href="https://www.dukeupress.edu/National-Abjection/" target="_blank">Karen Shimakawa has argued&#9873;<span class="popuptext">Shimakawa, Karen. 2002. <em>National Abjection: The Asian American Body Onstage</em>. Durham, NC: Duke University Press. 26.</span></a> serves not only as a projection of desire onto a Orientalized Other but also <a class="popup-comment" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title="Shimakawa's <em>National Abjection</em>" data-bs-content='Shimakawa is discussing, in particular, David Henry Hwang’s play <em>M. Butterfly</em> and what she sees as the role of white men’s desire for feminized Asian men in “serving the dual (or colluded) purposes of abjecting Asianness and the traumatic memory of military defeat” in the Vietnam War.'>an attempt to psychologically abject the national shame of the failures of those imperial projects.<sup><b>&#8224;</b></sup></a> <a class="popup-citation" onmouseover="myFunction(event)" onmouseout="myFunction(event)" href="https://nyupress.org/9780814759400/brown-boys-and-rice-queens/" target="_blank">“[T]he rice queen,” in the words of Eng-Beng Lim, is “in a hyperbolic state of self-induced Asian possession”&#9873;<span class="popuptext">Lim, Eng-Beng. 2013. <em>Brown Boys and Rice Queens: Spellbinding Performance in the Asias</em>. New York: New York University Press. 157.</span></a> – a possession which is also fundamentally consumptive.</p>
    <p>So persistent has this rhetoric been that even oppositional discourses have had to take its language: from <a class="popup-citation" onmouseover="myFunction(event)" onmouseout="myFunction(event)" href="https://tupress.temple.edu/books/20000000009702" target="_blank">“sticky rice” as a descriptor for intra-racial Asian relationships&#9873;<span class="popuptext">Wu, Cindy. 2018. <em>Sticky Rice: A Politics of Intraracial Desire</em>. Philadelphia: Temple University Press.</span></a> to Hoang Tan Nguyen’s framing of <a class="popup-hyperlink" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title="Explore Further" data-bs-content="<a href='/Sonic-Unreal/gaysianness'>Gaysianness - Identity, Affect, Aesthetic</a>" style="text-decoration: underline; font-weight: bold;">Gaysian</a> sexuality as a <a class="popup-citation" onmouseover="myFunction(event)" onmouseout="myFunction(event)" href="https://www.dukeupress.edu/a-view-from-the-bottom" target="_blank">“politics of starch.”&#9873;<span class="popuptext">Nguyen, Hoang Tan. 2014. “The Politics of Starch.” In <em>A View from the Bottom: Asian American Masculinity and Sexual Representation</em>. Durham, NC: Duke University Press.</span></a> Alongside the continued proliferation of “no fats, femmes, or Asians,” rice is the predominant mode through which queer Asian American sexuality is represented. Desires for and of queer Asianness are understood as consumptive desires, in which eroticism is inherently <a class="popup-hyperlink" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title="Explore Further" data-bs-content="<a href='/Sonic-Unreal/ingestion'>Ingestion</a>" style="text-decoration: underline; font-weight: bold;">ingestive</a>.</p>
    <p>The positioning of queer Asian bodies as objects for majoritarian consumption is a constant source of epistemic and physical violence for Gaysians globally. Even as I am writing this, a white German woman’s TikTok video is going viral for showing L’gie Bloomz, a drag queen in Bangkok, cooking a meal in a boiling pot of water on top of her head while she performs, pulling ingredients off her outfit to add to the pot. The video was posted without any reference to the performer’s identity and then reposted by media outlets with the framing language of documentary film; PinkNews captioned the video as “Spectacular footage captured by TikTok user pasteltravels,” rhetorically positioning it a glimpse into the reality of queer Thai life. Bloomz’s performance is stripped of context, her queer Asian body reduced to its material and ontological adjacency to food and greeted by social media comments like “No one does it like Thailand.”</p>
    <p>And yet, consumption need not be an enactment of majoritarian violence. We might draw from Vivian Huang, who argues that <a class="popup-citation" onmouseover="myFunction(event)" onmouseout="myFunction(event)" href="https://www.dukeupress.edu/surface-relations" target="_blank">“[d]iscourses that promote hospitality… without acknowledging the historic power differentials that accompany their circulation risk once more putting the burden of ‘saying yes’ on women of color most of all.”&#9873;<span class="popuptext">Huang, Vivian L. 2022. <em>Surface Relations: Queer Forms of Asian American Inscrutability</em>. Durham, NC: Duke University Press. 53.</span></a> Huang refuses to relinquish the possibilities for hospitality as a generative queer of color practice, however; they seek <a class="popup-citation" onmouseover="myFunction(event)" onmouseout="myFunction(event)" href="https://www.dukeupress.edu/surface-relations" target="_blank">“a feminist and Asian strategy to expand the submissive position, to position oneself to accept and receive, to risk the optic of submissiveness, and to politicize that embrace.”&#9873;<span class="popuptext">Huang, Vivian L. 2022. Surface Relations: Queer Forms of Asian American Inscrutability. Durham, NC: Duke University Press. 72.</span></a></p>
    <p>Similarly, the consumption of <a class="popup-hyperlink" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title="Explore Further" data-bs-content="<a href='/Sonic-Unreal/gaysianness'>Gaysianness - Identity, Affect, Aesthetic</a>" style="text-decoration: underline; font-weight: bold;">Gaysianness</a> need not - and, indeed, should not and is not - be overdetermined by the <a class="popup-citation" onmouseover="myFunction(event)" onmouseout="myFunction(event)" href="https://www.beacon.org/Culture-and-Truth-Revised-P352.aspx" target="_blank">“imperialist nostalgia”&#9873;<span class="popuptext">Rosaldo, Renato. 1993. <em>Culture and Truth: The Remaking of Social Analysis</em>. Revised Edition. Boston: Beacon Press.</span></a> that undergirds many of its forms. Positioning oneself as a queer of color subject open for consensual, generative consumption can be <a class="popup-hyperlink" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title="Explore Further" data-bs-content="<a href='/Sonic-Unreal/lets-never-live-in-fear'>Let's Never Live in Fear</a>" style="text-decoration: underline; font-weight: bold;">a practice of radical vulnerability and immense pleasure</a>. Whether in <a class="popup-citation" onmouseover="myFunction(event)" onmouseout="myFunction(event)" href="https://archive.org/details/breathlesserotic00tsui" target="_blank">Chinese American lesbian writer Kitty Tsui’s erotica where having sex and eating food blur into one another&#9873;<span class="popuptext">Tsui, Kitty. 1996. <em>Breathless</em>. Ithaca, NY: Firebrand Books.</span></a>, or in <a class="popup-hyperlink" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title="Explore Further" data-bs-content="<a href='/Sonic-Unreal/dragon-fruit'>Dragon Fruit</a>" style="text-decoration: underline; font-weight: bold;">Miss Shu Mai’s drag performances as food where audiences are invited to imagine eating her</a> - the choice to open oneself up to another, and the rich aesthetics of such practices, acknowledges that you and I were never quite distinct to begin with, that <a class="popup-hyperlink" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title="Explore Further" data-bs-content="<a href='/Sonic-Unreal/porousness-of-the-self'>The Porousness of the Self</a>" style="text-decoration: underline; font-weight: bold;">the porousness between our selves</a> enables a radical, erotic vulnerability.</p>
