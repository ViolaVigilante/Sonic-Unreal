    <p><a class="popup-citation" onmouseover="myFunction(event)" onmouseout="myFunction(event)" href="https://tupress.temple.edu/books/q-a-2" target="_blank">“Food is intrinsically linked to identity, budding as an ‘immigrant act,’”&#9873;<span class="popuptext">Sookkasikon, Pahole. 2021. “‘Save the Thai Temple’: Wat Mongkolratanaram, Thai America, and the Heteronormative Logics of South Berkeley.” In <em>Q&A: Voices from Queer Asian North America</em>, edited by Martin F. Manalansan IV, Alice Y. Hom, and Kale Bantigue Fajardo, 301–9. Philadelphia: Temple University Press.</span></a> as Pahole Sookkasikon, drawing on <a class="popup-citation" onmouseover="myFunction(event)" onmouseout="myFunction(event)" href="https://www.dukeupress.edu/immigrant-acts" target="_blank">Lisa Lowe,&#9873;<span class="popuptext">Lowe, Lisa. 1996. <em>Immigrant Acts: On Asian American Cultural Politics</em>. Durham, NC: Duke University Press.</span></a> argues in the context of Thai communities in California. Food serves as both a microcosm of and a contributor to political discourses of racialization and immigration in the United States. <a class="popup-citation" onmouseover="myFunction(event)" onmouseout="myFunction(event)" href="https://uhpress.hawaii.edu/title/dubious-gastronomy-the-cultural-politics-of-eating-asian-in-the-usa/" target="_blank">Debates around authenticity and hybridity in food map neatly onto parallel debates regarding the American body politic&#9873;<span class="popuptext">Ku, Robert Ji-Song. 2014. <em>Dubious Gastronomy: The Cultural Politics of Eating Asian in the USA</em>. Honolulu: University of Hawai’i Press.</span></a> – itself a metaphor which invites speculation on what the body politic must ingest and excrete for its survival. In order to understand the place of food in the Gaysian nightlife scene in <a class="popup-hyperlink" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title="Explore Further" data-bs-content="<a href='/Sonic-Unreal/global-greater-los-angeles'>Global Greater Los Angeles</a>" style="text-decoration: underline; font-weight: bold;">global greater Los Angeles</a>, it is crucial to map the broader <a class="popup-hyperlink" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title="Explore Further" data-bs-content="<a href='/Sonic-Unreal/consumptive-construction-of-gaysianness'>The Consumptive Construction of Gaysianness</a>" style="text-decoration: underline; font-weight: bold;">ingestive constructions of queer Asianness within US empire</a> with which these performances playfully disidentify.</p>
    <div class="container">
        <div class="row">
            <div class="col-7">It is not only contemporary Gaysianness that has taken shape in relation to this social terrain of simultaneous abjection and fetishization, rejection and consumption. <a class="popup-citation" onmouseover="myFunction(event)" onmouseout="myFunction(event)" href="https://nyupress.org/9780814770030/racial-indigestion/" target="_blank">Whiteness and American national identity themselves have, over centuries, been consolidated through the consumption of racial alterity.&#9873;<span class="popuptext">Tompkins, Kyla Wazana. 2012. <em>Racial Indigestion: Eating Bodies in the 19th Century</em>. New York: New York University Press.</span></a> While <a class="popup-citation" onmouseover="myFunction(event)" onmouseout="myFunction(event)" href="https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262029575/the-stack/" target="_blank">some political theorists have argued for an ingestive/digestive understanding of nation states generally,&#9873;<span class="popuptext">Bratton, Benjamin H. 2016. <em>The Stack: On Software and Sovereignty</em>. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press. 22.</span></a> these discourses took on particular currency in the context of Asian immigration to the United States during the 19th and early 20th centuries. Political commentators throughout this period framed immigration as an issue of digestion, in which immigrants would either be successfully dissolved into the melting pot of American national identity or would turn the tables, ingesting America before America could ingest them.</div>

    <p>These ingestive concerns have shifted with the rise of neoliberal multiculturalism in the United States, with the consumption of Otherness becoming as much an aspect of individualized behavior as national political debate. <a class="popup-citation" onmouseover="myFunction(event)" onmouseout="myFunction(event)" href="https://www.wiley.com/en-us/media-and-cultural-studies-keyworks-2nd-edition-p-9780470658086" target="_blank">bell hooks famously referred to this consumptive urge as “eating the other,” critiquing multicultural consumerism as imperialist nostalgia with “a distinctly postmodern slant.”&#9873;<span class="popuptext">hooks, bell. 1990. “Eating the Other: Desire and Resistance.” In <em>Media and Cultural Studies: KeyWorks</em>, edited by Douglas M. Kellner and Meenakshi Gigi Durham, Revised Edition, 366. Malden, MA: Blackwell Publishing.</span></a> Food studies scholars have <a class="popup-comment" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title='Ethnic Food' data-bs-content='See, for example:<br><br><a target="_blank" href="https://doi.org/10.1080/15528014.2017.1398470">Hulme, Alison. 2018. “Importing the Night Market: Urban Regeneration and the Asian Food Aesthetic in London.” <em>Food, Culture & Society</em> 21(1):42–54.</a><br><br><a target="_blank" href="https://doi.org/10.2307/4129222">Turgeon, Laurier, and Madeleine Pastinelli. 2002. “‘Eat the World’: Postcolonial Encounters in Quebec City’s Ethnic Restaurants.” <em>Journal of American Folklore</em> 115(456):247–68.</a>'>written extensively<sup><b>&#8224;</b></sup></a> on the literalization of this eating in the realm of “ethnic food,” wherein cosmopolitan consumers can not only taste <em>as</em> racialized Others do but also, in so doing, consume that very Otherness in order to be reinterpellated into their own privileged positions. Even broad debates over Asian American assimilation continue to be articulated in ingestive terms, with scholars reading <a class="popup-citation" onmouseover="myFunction(event)" onmouseout="myFunction(event)" href="https://nyupress.org/9781479869251/eating-asian-america/" target="_blank">failures of the model minority myth and refusals of assimilation as incomplete consumption&#9873;<span class="popuptext">Manalansan IV, Martin F. 2013. “Beyond Authenticity: Rerouting the Filipino Culinary Diaspora.” In <em>Eating Asian America: A Food Studies Reader</em>, edited by Robert Ji-Song Ku, Martin F. Manalansan IV, and Anita Mannur, 288–300. New York: New York University Press.</span></a>; as Tao Leigh Goffe has argued, <a class="popup-citation" onmouseover="myFunction(event)" onmouseout="myFunction(event)" href="https://doi.org/10.1080/0740770X.2020.1791384" target="_blank">“the United States continually fails to digest its racialized others.”&#9873;<span class="popuptext">Goffe, Tao Leigh. 2020. “Chop Suey Surplus: Chinese Food, Sex, and the Political Economy of Afro-Asia.” <em>Women & Performance: A Journal of Feminist Theory</em> 30(1):23.</span></a></p>
