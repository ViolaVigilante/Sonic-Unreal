    <p>In 2012, Kyla Wazana Tompkins coined the term “critical eating studies” to refer to scholarship which <a class="popup-citation" onmouseover="myFunction(event)" onmouseout="myFunction(event)" href="https://nyupress.org/9780814770030/racial-indigestion/" target="_blank">“look[s] beyond food itself to consider practices of ingestion and edibility.”&#9873;<span class="popuptext">Tompkins, Kyla Wazana. 2012. <em>Racial Indigestion: Eating Bodies in the 19th Century</em>. New York: New York University Press. 2.</span></a> Tompkins sought to move beyond the object-oriented approach dominant in much of food studies, even with that field’s long-standing attempts to turn towards <a class="popup-comment" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title='Foodways' data-bs-content='“Foodways” as a term attempts to bring together the material, economic, cultural, and political dimensions of food. It is an expansive (and often poorly-defined) term which can include everything from the literary study of cookbooks to the economics of international food shipping. Historically, foodways have been approached in the scholarly literature as coeval with (what are perceived to be) well-bounded cultural groups, such that one might study “Jewish foodways” or “Vietnamese foodways.”<br><br>Mark Padoongpatt offers a concise definition: “the production, representation, and consumption of a food” <a target="_blank" href="https://nyupress.org/9781479869251/eating-asian-america/">(2013)</a>.'>“foodways”<sup><b>&#8224;</b></sup></a> as a primary analytic. In her work, Tompkins explored <a class="popup-citation" onmouseover="myFunction(event)" onmouseout="myFunction(event)" href="https://nyupress.org/9780814770030/racial-indigestion/" target="_blank">“eating as a racial performative act”<b>&#9873;</b><span class="popuptext">Tompkins, Kyla Wazana. 2012. <em>Racial Indigestion: Eating Bodies in the 19th Century</em>. New York: New York University Press. 11.</span></a> throughout the history of the United States; she helps show how dominant US understandings of race, nationality, and the state are dependent upon the language of eating, consumption, and <a class="popup-hyperlink" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title="Explore Further" data-bs-content="<a href='/Sonic-Unreal/ingestion'>Ingestion</a>" style="text-decoration: underline; font-weight: bold;">ingestion</a>.</p>
    <p>Critical eating studies – with its explicit emphasis on politics, <a class="popup-hyperlink" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title="Explore Further" data-bs-content="<a href='/Sonic-Unreal/performance-theory'>Performance Theory</a>" style="text-decoration: underline; font-weight: bold;">performativity</a>, and the philosophical implications of consumption – offers a framework for triangulating the ingestive between material, discursive, and historical contexts. As such, it is a necessary tool for fully understanding <a class="popup-hyperlink" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title="Explore Further" data-bs-content="<a href='/Sonic-Unreal/food-in-gaysian-nightlife'>Food in Gaysian Nightlife</a>" style="text-decoration: underline; font-weight: bold;">the importance of food to Gaysian nightlife in contemporary Los Angeles</a>. We must attend not only to the physical presence of food in nightlife spaces; nor only to practices of cooking, eating, and drinking in these spaces; nor only to lyrical and metaphorical invocations of foods and tastes. All of these are critical but non-exhaustive elements of a larger sociocultural framework which rests upon deeper ideas of <a class="popup-hyperlink" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title="Explore Further" data-bs-content="<a href='/Sonic-Unreal/ingestion'>Ingestion</a>" style="text-decoration: underline; font-weight: bold;">ingestion</a>, <a class="popup-hyperlink" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title="Explore Further" data-bs-content="<a href='/Sonic-Unreal/consumptive-construction-of-gaysianness'>The Consumptive Construction of Gaysianness</a>" style="text-decoration: underline; font-weight: bold;">consumption</a>, and <a class="popup-hyperlink" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title="Explore Further" data-bs-content="<a href='/Sonic-Unreal/metabolism'>Metabolism</a>" style="text-decoration: underline; font-weight: bold;">metabolism</a>.</p>
    <p>A significant amount of scholarship in Asian American studies builds, even if indirectly, upon work in critical eating studies. As such, one of my goals is to bring together <a class="popup-hyperlink" data-bs-toggle="popover" data-bs-html="true" data-bs-title="Explore Further" data-bs-content="<a href='/Sonic-Unreal/food-and-queer-asian-america'>Food and (Queer) Asian America</a>" style="text-decoration: underline; font-weight: bold;">critical perspectives on eating, consumption, and ingestion in Asian American politics and cultural production</a> under the umbrella of critical eating studies, even if the original authors do not use this term to describe their work. We might think of Anne Anlin Cheng’s attempts to theorize <a class="popup-citation" onmouseover="myFunction(event)" onmouseout="myFunction(event)" href="https://global.oup.com/academic/product/ornamentalism-9780190604615" target="_blank">“the ontology of sushi eating… to reconsider, or even remap, some of the most fundamental terms of our sociality and our humanness,”&#9873;<span class="popuptext">Cheng, Anne Anlin. 2019. <em>Ornamentalism</em>. New York: Oxford University Press. 109.</span></a> or of Anita Mannur’s interrogation of how <a class="popup-citation" onmouseover="myFunction(event)" onmouseout="myFunction(event)" href="https://www.dukeupress.edu/intimate-eating" target="_blank">“food [can] be used - not to tell us what people eat per se, but to illuminate how radical publics and intimate spaces of belonging (and unbelonging) are created for nonnormative subjects.”&#9873;<span class="popuptext">Mannur, Anita. 2022. <em>Intimate Eating: Racialized Spaces and Radical Futures</em>. Durham, NC: Duke University Press. 4.</span></a></p>
